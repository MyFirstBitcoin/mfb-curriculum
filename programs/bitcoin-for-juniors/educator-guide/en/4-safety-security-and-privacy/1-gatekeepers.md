# 4.2 Gatekeepers

_Discussion, Sorting, Reflection · Whole class, Pairs, Individual · 30 min_

Students explore the difference between public and private spaces and information through guided discussion and visual examples. Using familiar everyday situations, learners practice classifying what is usually public or private and reflect on the idea that individuals can choose what information they keep private or share. The activity builds early awareness of privacy, boundaries, and personal responsibility.

#### Objectives

**Learning Outcomes**

By the end of this activity, learners will:

* Distinguish between public and private spaces and information
* Explain why some things should be kept private
* Recognize that people can choose what they share
* Practice respectful discussion and personal decision-making

**Objective In Student’s Words**

*  “Assess what things are public, what things are private, and what I want to share.”

#### Materials

* Visual aids or flash cards showing examples of places and information (See Appendix A)
* Student worksheet with checkboxes for Public / Private (See Appendix B)
* Pencils or crayons
* Whiteboard or poster board
* Markers
* Nice-to-have: Projector or screen for images

#### Setup

* Display the example cards or slides and write **PUBLIC** and **PRIVATE** on the board.
* Set out worksheets and writing materials.

#### Procedure

##### Step 1: Set up the lesson

**5 minutes**

1. Ask students, “What does ‘public’ mean?” and “What does ‘private’ mean?” Accept multiple answers without correcting them yet, then explain that the class will decide together using everyday examples.

##### Step 2: Activity

**20 minutes**

###### Setup

1. Explain that some things are usually public, some are usually private, and some depend on choice—so there is not always one correct answer.

###### Model

1. Compare a public example, such as a park (“Many people can go there and see it”), with a private example, such as a password (“This belongs only to one person”).

###### Carry Out

1. Show one visual example at a time—such as a park, bus, washroom, car, eye colour, password, or bank PIN—and have students vote ‘public’ or ‘private.’ Place it under the matching heading, then ask “Why?” or “Could this ever change?”

###### Checkpoint

1. Ask students to explain the difference between public and private in their own words; listen for understanding rather than memorized definitions.

###### Reflection

* Ask: “Is eye colour always private?”
* Ask: “Can something be private even if other people can see it?”
* Guide students toward the idea that people choose what information they share.

##### Step 3: Wrap up

1. Collect worksheets and materials. Reinforce: “You get to decide what information you share; being smart about privacy helps keep you safe.”

#### Key points

* Distinguish public and private information and decide what to share.

#### Discussion questions

**5 minutes**

1. Distribute the checkbox worksheet.
1. Students decide for each item whether they would keep it public or private.
1. Emphasize that answers may be different for different people.

#### Notes

###### Classroom management

* Encourage respectful listening and differing opinions
* Avoid asking students to share sensitive personal information

###### Extensions & Sponge Activities

* Add digital examples such as usernames or photos
* Create a class rule poster for sharing information
* Look at superheroes and villains - why do they protect their identities?
* Make a diagram of information each student wants to share/keep private

###### Differentiation

* Younger students: use only place-based examples
* Older students: move to more difficult subjects (DNA, Facial Recognition, GPS location, Name)
* ELL/Accessibility: visuals, gestures, simple language
* Safety: avoid asking for real passwords or personal data

###### Suggested assignment

Ask a trusted adult to name one kind of information they choose to keep private and why. Share one thing you learned about public and private information.
