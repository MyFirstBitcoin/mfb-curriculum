# 1.2 Going, Going, Gone!

_Role play, Auction · Whole class · 45 min_

In this activity, students take part in a classroom auction using “shells” (or tokens) as a simple form of money. The class runs a live auction where everyday objects are sold one at a time to the highest bidder. As prices emerge in real time, students observe how scarcity (limited items and limited money) and demand (how many people want the same item) shape what something “costs.” A guided reflection links their auction outcomes to core ideas in value, exchange, and price formation, emphasizing that prices are not fixed and that different people value the same object differently.

#### Objectives

**Learning Outcomes**

* Students experience [scarcity](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#scarcity) and [price](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#price) formation by participating in a live auction.
* They learn that objects gain [value](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#value) depending on how many people want them, and how limited they are.
* Students understand that:
  * Scarcity makes items more valuable.
  * Prices are not fixed; they depend on demand.
  * People value different things differently.

**Objective In Student’s Words**

* Use shells to bid in an auction and see how prices change when lots of people want the same thing and there aren’t many of them.

#### Materials

* 10 small classroom objects (fun or useful: stickers, pencils, snacks, erasers, funny hats, toys, etc.)
* Shells (or paper tokens/coins if no shells are available) — at least 3–5 per student
* Whiteboard or chart paper and marker for a bid record visible to the whole class
* Nice-to-have: Auction bell or clap/signal

#### Setup

* Count each student's starting tokens and place the 10 auction items on a front table.

#### Procedure

##### Step 1: Set up the lesson

**5 minutes**

1. Show the auction items, naming unfamiliar ones, then give out the shells as the money for today’s auction and have students count them.

##### Step 2: Activity

**30 minutes**

###### Setup

1. Explain that items are sold one at a time to the highest shell bid.

###### Model

1. Run one practice bid: start at one shell, take a higher bid, make bids visible, then close with “Going once, going twice, sold,” announce the price, and record it.

###### Live Auction

1. Auction each item from one shell; record its final price where everyone can see it.

###### Checkpoints

* Remind students to budget: they cannot bid after their shells are gone.

##### Step 3: Wrap up

* Give items to the winners and congratulate the class.

#### Key points

* Participate in an auction to explore how scarcity and demand influence price.

#### Discussion questions

1. Lay all items with their final prices on a board/wall.
1. Ask:
1. Which items were most expensive? Why?
1. Were some things not worth much? Why?
1. Did everyone want the same things?
1. Did anyone run out of shells too quickly?
1. Highlight: Price comes from value _and_ scarcity.
1. Define these terms and write them on the board.

#### Notes

###### Classroom management

* Some students may find this concept unfair. They may have been outbid on items they wanted. Be aware that this could be a negative learning experience. Offer the possibility of further trade and [barter](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#barter) (see _Differentiation_ below).

###### Differentiation

* Modulating the distribution of tokens can change the outcome of the activity. The simplest and fairest method is to distribute the tokens equally. However, this is not the most realistic situation. Unequal distribution may be suitable with more advanced students to discuss financial mobility and inequality.
* Younger students: If bidding is not easy for students, it is possible to ask around the group in turn.
* ELL/Accessibility: Children can indicate their bid by placing shells at the centre of the table, poker-style.

###### Extensions & Sponge Activities

* If no actual giveaway: instead of giving the items away, students write on post-its what they think each item _should_ cost. Compare their “predictions” with the actual auction results.
* This makes the activity more theoretical and allows for repeating without needing prizes.
* Time permitting, a trade market can be opened up with students who are not satisfied with the auction. Students can be encouraged to barter and use any remaining shells to trade with each other. This can either be structured and led via the teacher, or decentralized and sporadic, allowing students to take the initiative.

###### Suggested assignment

Tell a parent or caregiver about one auction item and why its price changed. Ask what they think can make a price go up or down.
