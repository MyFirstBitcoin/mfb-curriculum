# What is Bitcoin?

Before Bitcoin, sending money online always depended on a trusted organization: a bank or payment company that kept the ledger, approved accounts, and could delay, block, or reverse a payment. For decades, computer scientists tried to create digital money without a central issuer. The hard part was that digital information is easy to copy. If a digital coin could be copied and spent twice, it could not work as money. This is called the **double-spend problem**.

On 31 October 2008, a person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a paper describing a solution: _Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System_. The network started in January 2009.

Bitcoin is digital money that is **decentralized**, has a **limited supply**, and can be sent **peer to peer** without a bank's permission.

Being **decentralized** means there is no central office that can create accounts, change balances, or approve who joins. Anyone can download and inspect the open-source software, and many independent computers keep a copy of the record. This does not mean everyone has equal influence. It means no single participant can change the ledger or the monetary rules for everyone.

Having a **limited supply** means there will never be more than 21 million bitcoin. New bitcoin are released on a public schedule, and independent computers called nodes reject any block that breaks that rule. The limit is credible because anyone can check it. It is not a law of nature, though: it holds because participants keep enforcing it. And a limited supply does not guarantee that the price will rise. Price depends on demand as well as supply.

Being **peer to peer** means you can send bitcoin to anyone, anywhere, without opening an account with the Bitcoin network or asking a central authority for approval. No company can freeze bitcoin that you control yourself. The other side of that freedom is responsibility: a valid transaction is usually impossible to reverse, and there is no help desk for the network.

One misconception is that Bitcoin is out of reach because one bitcoin is expensive. **You do not need to buy or use a whole bitcoin.** One bitcoin divides into 100,000,000 smaller units called **satoshis**, or **sats**. Owning 100,000 sats and owning 0.001 bitcoin describe the same amount.

The network relies on different participants: **users** who send, receive, and hold bitcoin; **nodes** that check every transaction and block against the rules; and **miners** who compete to add a new block to the record about every ten minutes. That record, the **blockchain**, is public and visible to anyone, and it is very costly to rewrite.

A note on spelling: **Bitcoin** with a capital B usually means the network or the system. **bitcoin** with a lowercase b means the money itself.

Bitcoin also has trade-offs. Its price can change quickly. Mistakes are usually permanent. Acceptance is still limited in many places, and using it safely takes some learning. Bitcoin replaces some trust in institutions with verification and personal responsibility. It does not remove risk.
