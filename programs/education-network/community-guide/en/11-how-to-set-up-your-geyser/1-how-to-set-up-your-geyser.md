# 9.1 How to Set up Your Geyser

In the spirit of open source everything, this chapter is a complete guide for anyone who wants to run a Bitcoin Educators Unconference. It is based on the unconferences My First Bitcoin has run since 2023, including what went well and what we would do differently.

An unconference is not a regular conference. Imagine a safe place where like-valued people gather, share ideas, brainstorm deeply and build their network. The agenda is created together, partly on the day itself, by the people in the room. Some general references for inspiration:

* [Organize a Local BarCamp](http://barcamp.org/w/page/404135/OrganizeALocalBarCamp)
* [Unconference methods](https://unconference.net/methods-2/)
* [Internet Identity Workshop](https://internetidentityworkshop.com/) (a long-running unconference)

If you have not run an event before, start with a meetup (see _How to Organize a Meetup_). An unconference is a bigger step: plan at least three months ahead.

**Templates:** a [planning template](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1iQInaC3tvlsvlmxepKtXuIEN_8_e4QlG/view) (spreadsheet), a [week-by-week checklist](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1w_BVR8qm4ycZhiifCZ-qamrHLSAmjblZ/view) from a real event, and two printable posters: the [unconference SOP](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1mMccxzD4yqVHGKwwCbf8vXNQyUiBtGfv/view) and the [planning overview](https://drive.google.com/file/d/15fG62qv7qOrLXF7cfs4HLDZ-Dp8y8Gp7/view). Everything is in the [Unconference folder](https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1gP0_PwLHkNNLmWywZia5xfXlPwRf__NE). Make a copy and adapt it.

## 1. Preparation and first decisions

These decisions are not a complete list, but each one materially shapes how you plan and run the event. Think them through before anything else.

### Timing: align with another conference, or not

This decision sets the direction for the rest of your planning. Talk with the organisers of the conferences most relevant to you.

* Our 2023 unconference in El Salvador was the day before Adopting Bitcoin. Our 2024 unconference in Nashville was the day before a large Bitcoin conference.
* Smaller conferences often match the vibe and audience of an unconference better. Larger ones bring more people, but also competing side events fighting for the same attention and time.
* A third option is to hold the unconference on its own, not tied to any other event.

### Choose an event lead

Recruiting a project lead is essential to coordinate planning and execution. See _Roles and responsibilities_ below for the profile that suits this role best.

### Affiliated with My First Bitcoin, or independent

Anyone can run an unconference, on any subject, at any time. You have complete freedom for an independent event. Partnering with My First Bitcoin has advantages: shared lessons learned, shared audiences, and subject experts from the Education Network. The condition is that the event follows the network's principles:

* Independent
* Impartial
* Community-based
* Bitcoin only
* Empowerment over profits
* Quality

If you want to partner, contact us before you start planning: arnold@myfirstbitcoin.org.

### No sponsorships

My First Bitcoin does not take sponsorships, and we strongly discourage them for unconferences. It is tempting to accept a sponsor for the event, or for part of it. The risk is strings attached: influence over who speaks, the venue, the marketing. Sponsors have their own incentives.

A sponsor can also make it look as if the event endorses a company, brand or philosophy, which conflicts with independence. Donations carry far less of this risk. See _Funding & Sustainability_.

### Partner with other Communities

Working with Communities in the Education Network makes planning and running an unconference much easier. A local Community knows the culture, can scout venues and coordinate local vendors. In Nashville, Bitcoin veterans supported the event, which helped in many ways, including recruiting volunteers.

Find Communities near you on the [Education Network Leaderboard](https://leaderboard.myfirstbitcoin.org).

### The unconference ethos versus breaking even

The ideal is an event true to the unconference ethos that also breaks even. While planning, tension will grow between the mission and the finances. Inviting influencers with large audiences may sell more tickets, but it attracts a different audience. Inviting high-signal people you would love to work with fits the ethos better, but brings fewer attendees.

It is not either-or: you can mix a few invited speakers with sessions created on the day. Whatever you decide, make it clear before the team starts planning.

Our rule of thumb: the people who come are the people who should have been there. The ethos is what gives an unconference its character. Do not sacrifice it, not even for the finances.

### Branding

Keep the unconference as neutral as possible: as an organiser you are not there to sell products to attendees, and the same goes for the event's branding. If you use the My First Bitcoin name or logo, follow the [Brand Book](https://github.com/MyFirstBitcoin/Brand-Book/), which says how they may and may not be used.

## 2. Roles and responsibilities

Clarify the team structure early. It prevents frustration later, makes clear who does what, avoids tasks falling through the cracks because everyone thought someone else had it, and avoids two people believing they are in charge of the same thing.

The event lead sets the planning rhythm and recruits the team. One person can hold several roles. We recommend a weekly planning call.

* **Executive director:** approves the decisions the team recommends, and recruits key team members, such as the local lead.
* **Event lead (project lead):** runs daily operations. Comfortable building action lists, running planning sessions, assigning tasks and following up. Meets the executive director early to agree responsibilities, such as spending limits and speaker selection.
* **Local lead:** guides the team on local customs and culture, finds and screens local vendors, and visits the venue in person. Check for Communities nearby to collaborate with.
* **Tech lead:** builds the website and runs ticket sales and other tech. If this person cannot attend in person, the event lead recruits an audio-visual lead.
* **Audio-visual (AV) lead:** plans, sources and runs microphones, projectors, cameras, lights and other equipment. Raises options with the team, such as whether to livestream (which changes the internet requirements).
* **Marketing and communications:** approves all communication on the website, socials and outreach. Takes part in planning, such as speaker selection, so announcements go out on time.
* **Accounting:** handles all payments, invoices and refunds with the event lead. Guides the team on budget limits, accounting processes and regulations. Make sure whoever books services asks for tax exemption, and shares your nonprofit certificate when needed.
* **MC:** runs the day: announcements, and the session pitches at the start. Builds enough excitement that attendees share their session ideas, and moderates to keep out long introductions, sales pitches and personal agendas (such as job searching). Leads the scheduling of sessions and tells session leaders and attendees. With several spaces, assign an MC per space.
* **Creatives:** designers who make what you need: slides, flyers, announcements.
* **Volunteers:** extra hands are invaluable, especially on the day: the sign-in desk, setting up chairs and tables, audio-visual, clean-up. In Nashville a team of 10 volunteers each wore an event shirt, so attendees could spot them, and stayed in touch through a Signal group.

## 3. Finances

There are two ways to plan the finances. Set a budget and let the team work within it, or build the event from the bottom up: estimate costs and expected ticket sales, and adjust ticket prices to cover the expenses.

Agree early who may spend how much, and how vendors get paid: does the local lead pay and get reimbursed, or do all invoices go to one organisation?

### Revenue

* Donations
* Fundraising on the day
* Ticket sales: early bird, standard, discounted (students, veterans or other groups), last minute
* Sponsorships (discouraged, see above)

### Headcount

* Estimated sales per ticket type (count toward headcount)
* Speakers and invited guests (count toward headcount, not ticket sales)
* Staff and volunteers (count toward headcount, not ticket sales)
* Estimated no-shows (count toward ticket sales, not the final headcount)

### Venue expenses

* Location: security deposit, proof of insurance
* Physical equipment: tents, stage, tables, chairs, whiteboards, markers
* Audio-visual equipment: microphones, mixers, speakers, projectors
* Food: catering or a food truck, snacks and desserts
* Drinks: coffee, tea, water, and other beverages if you offer them
* Staff: for example bar staff
* Ask every vendor for tax exemption for your nonprofit status

### Marketing expenses

* Shirts for staff and volunteers
* Posters, for example the schedule
* Wristbands
* Stickers or QR labels for fundraising on the day
* Banners

### Other expenses

* Travel, lodging and incidentals
* Contracted staff, such as a videographer
* A speakers' dinner after the event (optional)

## 4. Venue and session strategy

Decide the mix of talks, panels and sessions created on the day before you book a venue. This is not about choosing the speakers yet: it is about knowing what you need from a venue, such as the number and size of rooms.

More sessions is not always better. With too many, attendees feel torn between sessions and the conversations they are having, and skip sessions they care about because of clashes. How much time between sessions? How many at once?

### Questions to answer

* Will there be dedicated time to network?
* Will sessions run at the same time, and how will you schedule them?
* Will sessions use microphones? (Think about noise between spaces.)
* Is it a half day, a full day, or several days?
* What is the smallest session you will add to the schedule on the day?
* What is the largest session? Will everyone attend the opening?
* How many people in total, including staff, volunteers and guests?

### Venue search criteria

* The date (see the timing decision above)
* Size and rooms needed
* Distance to other conferences, if you align with one
* Budget (see venue expenses)
* Other needs, such as on-site food and drinks

## 5. Tech

Good technical support sounds obvious, but it takes more work than you might expect if you are new to events.

* **Website:** give the event its own page, with a clear standalone address. Our first unconferences used a page on the My First Bitcoin website; a separate address proved clearer for attendees.
* **Ticket sales:** choose a ticket system that supports discount codes (rather than separate pages for students or veterans), several tickets in one order, and receipts. Plan extra time for communicating with special groups.
* **Check it daily:** make sure your ticket site is online every day during the sale.
* **Promote it elsewhere:** ask partners to add a banner or pop-up for the event to their websites.

## 6. Marketing and branding

### Save the date

Plant the flag as soon as the venue is confirmed. See the week-by-week checklist for when each step happens.

### Where to announce

* Eventbrite, Meetup.com and the Orange Pill App
* Your own website and channels, and those of partner Communities
* The main conference's website, if your unconference is a side event

### Theme

Brand the event as a Bitcoin Educators Unconference, and explain how it differs from a regular conference: permissionless, decentralised, co-created.

### Content to prepare

* A press release (send it as a PDF)
* A post about the location
* Copy for the website
* A short trailer video
* Testimonials and footage from earlier events
* A flyer
* Throwback posts about previous unconferences
* Reminders while early bird tickets are still available
* Speaker announcements
* A call for session ideas, on socials and to everyone who has a ticket
* The schedule, once ready

Want examples from our past events (press release, website copy, flyers, photos, footage)? Write to arnold@myfirstbitcoin.org.

### Example: a call for speakers to ticket holders

_"Thank you for joining our Bitcoin Unconference on [date]. We are excited to welcome you at [venue]! This event is different from a regular conference: it is an unconference, which means we create the experience together. We welcome ideas for talks, specifically about Bitcoin education. Our team will review the submissions over the next few weeks. Please take a look at this form: [link]. There will also be a chance to pitch your idea at the start of the unconference itself. And if you simply want to attend and listen, that is fantastic too. P.S. Early bird tickets are almost gone, so if you know Bitcoiners who are on the fence, now is a good time to reach out. See you soon!"_

## 7. Operations

### Speakers and sessions

* Invite a few speakers who bring signal and fit the ethos, and open a speaker and workshop form on your website.
* Let a small committee review the applications together and build the schedule.
* Remember it is an unconference: it is fine, and intended, to leave space unfilled for sessions created on the day.

### Volunteers

* Explain the schedule and their responsibilities.
* Ask their shirt sizes in time.
* Ask for help capturing photos, videos and testimonials.

A volunteer duty list:

* Set up chairs, tablecloths and so on
* Check-in at arrival
* Audio help, such as the mixer
* Assist the MC, such as guiding people to sessions
* Collect testimonials
* Take photos and videos for future communication
* Fundraising on the day
* Tear-down and clean-up

### Tickets for the team

* Send tickets to staff, volunteers, speakers and invited guests.
* Check that paid tickets are sent automatically.

### Open source on the wall

To show the power of open source, we printed the [SOP](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1mMccxzD4yqVHGKwwCbf8vXNQyUiBtGfv/view) and the [planning overview](https://drive.google.com/file/d/15fG62qv7qOrLXF7cfs4HLDZ-Dp8y8Gp7/view) in large format and hung them at the event. Feel free to do the same.

## 8. Props kit

### Check-in table

* A phone or scanner to scan attendees' QR codes
* A laptop to check off arrivals
* A printed attendee list as a backup for the volunteers

### Merchandise

* Flyers and wristbands
* Shirts for staff and volunteers, so attendees can recognise them, and as a thank-you gift. Collect sizes in time!

### Other

* Banners
* Printed schedule posters
* A slideshow with the agenda for the main stage
* Microphones, projector and whiteboards (one per breakout room, if you can)

## 9. Timeline

A guide based on our [week-by-week checklist](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1w_BVR8qm4ycZhiifCZ-qamrHLSAmjblZ/view), for an event about three months out:

* **As soon as possible:** the first decisions (timing, event lead, affiliated or independent, ethos versus finances, branding), research venues, save the date, a one-pager.
* **Twelve to nine weeks before:** confirm local leads and support, budget and quotes, book the venue, set ticket prices, theme and collaborations, build the website and open ticket sales.
* **Eight to five weeks before:** press release, website copy, trailer and flyer, event insurance, book staff and equipment, open the speaker form, reach out to volunteers and send their tickets, start a volunteer chat group.
* **Four to two weeks before:** early bird reminders, throwback posts, speaker form to ticket holders, review speakers and prepare the schedule, book food, write the volunteer roles document.
* **The final week:** announce the schedule, email all attendees, prepare the slides background, print the schedule and posters, pack the props kit.
* **After the event:** thank speakers, send the feedback form, collect recordings and photos.

## 10. After the event

* Send a thank-you message to every speaker.
* Survey attendees and compile the feedback. Share it with the team before the review meeting.
* Collect recordings and photos from contractors and volunteers.
* Finalise the finances: expenses, ticket sales, donations, fundraising.
* Hold an after-action review (AAR) meeting with the planning team.
* Write down lessons learned, and share them so this guide keeps improving: arnold@myfirstbitcoin.org.

## 11. Lessons learned: Nashville 2024

### Marketing and ticket sales

* We competed with many overlapping events for the same attendees and the same speakers.
* Well-known names did not boost ticket sales as much as we expected.
* Using all social channels (LinkedIn, the Orange Pill App and others) worked well.
* A low ticket price was appreciated compared with formal events.

### The ticket website

* It needs to be reliable, simple, and on its own clear address.
* Allow several tickets in one order.
* Make it easy for media, volunteers and staff to get tickets.
* Use discount codes, not separate pages for veterans or students.
* Attendees wanted receipts.
* Check daily that the ticket site is online.

### The day's schedule

* Attendees felt torn between sessions and networking. Dedicated networking time, and fewer overlapping sessions, let people do both.
* Overlapping sessions were generally not received well.
* Noise from networking, and between floors of the venue, was distracting.
* Games were popular with some attendees, as fellowship and networking time.

### Sessions created on the day

* Pitches took longer than planned, and some drifted into personal backgrounds, job searches and topics outside education.
* Communicate the final schedule better: a phone-friendly page or app works better than one whiteboard.
* Put a whiteboard in every breakout room.

### Food and drinks

* The food truck was not communicated well to attendees, so we still had to pay its two-hour minimum.
* Snacks, coffee, tea and water worked well.
* Including drinks in the ticket price, rather than a cash bar, was appreciated.

### Finances

* We did not break even. Some costs, such as the food truck, are easy to cut. Selling more tickets, or at a higher price, is the other lever.
* We approved an extra projector on the day, and then struggled to agree its price. Agree prices in writing before you say yes.

### Venue

* Free street parking worked well.
* Check outdoor spaces for insects, and choose a venue with good sound insulation between floors.

**Action:** write down your first decisions (timing, event lead, affiliated or independent), then pick a date.
