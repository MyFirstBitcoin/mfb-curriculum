# Bitcoin Diploma

---

## Welcome to the Bitcoin Diploma

* My First Bitcoin: a nonprofit founded in El Salvador in 2021
* Free and open-source Bitcoin education for learners and educators
* First Diploma cohort: one public school in San Marcos, El Salvador, in 2022
* Focus: financial literacy, careful thinking and practical safety
* Not trading, speculation or promoting other tokens

<!-- notes: Introduce yourself and how you came to teach this Diploma. Stress that the course does not ask anyone to accept a belief: it gives concepts, questions and practical knowledge so learners can form their own conclusions. -->

---

## Our Mission and Values

**Independent, impartial and open-source Bitcoin education**

* **Accessibility:** in person, online or through print and PDF
* **Curiosity:** ask why, and admit what you do not know
* **Critical thinking:** check evidence, compare trade-offs
* **Community:** connect ideas to local life
* **Self-sovereignty:** more control means more responsibility

<!-- notes: Explain the three words of the mission. Independent: not controlled by a government, company or financial service. Impartial: evidence and trade-offs, no predetermined conclusion. Open-source: anyone can read, share, translate and improve it. -->

---

## Why This Diploma Exists

1. Explain the problem Bitcoin was designed to address
1. Explain Bitcoin's main response
1. Explain and practise the responsibility of holding and using bitcoin
1. Explore how Bitcoin may function as money in your community

<!-- notes: Most people use money before they learn how money works. The aim is not expertise in eight hours: it is a reliable foundation, avoiding common beginner mistakes, and preparing to keep learning. Learners do not need to buy bitcoin or install a wallet to complete the Diploma. -->

---

* **Module 1, Problem:** How can money break?

* **Module 3, Action:** How do I use and secure Bitcoin responsibly?

# Module 1: Understanding Money

You will explain the money problems Bitcoin responds to.

---

## Opening Questions About Money

* Money is familiar, but it is not simple.
* Why do people need money, and what makes something money?
* Who can create or control your money, and why does it have value?
* Does your money buy the same amount today as in the past?
* Notice the difference between a fact and a preference.

<!-- notes: Opening reflection, suggested time 10 minutes: individual writing, then cohort discussion. Ask learners to keep their answers and return to them at the end of the Diploma. Use the guide's example: "This note is accepted for taxes" is a fact that can be checked, while "This is the best form of money" is a conclusion that needs criteria and evidence. -->

---

## Why Money Exists

* Exchange lets people specialize: a farmer, a builder, a nurse.
* Direct exchange fails when the other person does not want what you offer.
* Money is accepted because people expect others to accept it later.
* It helps strangers cooperate across distance and time.
* Money is a tool for exchanging claims, not the valued thing itself.

<!-- notes: Emphasise that money solves a coordination problem and also gives a common way to record prices, debts, income, costs, and savings. Spot question: why did money come into existence, and what exchange or coordination problem does it help people solve? -->

---

## Functions and Properties of Money

* Medium of exchange: accepted in payment, reducing the need for barter.
* Unit of account: a shared unit for comparing prices and keeping records.
* Store of value: moves value from the present into the future.
* An item can perform one function better than another.

<!-- notes: Stress that no money stores value perfectly, but some forms lose purchasing power more slowly than others. Use the guide's example: a national currency may be widely accepted for daily payments while another asset is chosen for long-term saving. -->

---

<!-- _class: two-column -->

## Properties of Useful Money

* Durability: remains usable over time
* Portability: easy to move or transfer
* Divisibility: small and large amounts
* Fungibility: equal units are interchangeable
* Scarcity: supply is hard to increase
* Acceptability: others will receive it
* Verifiability: people can check it is genuine

<!-- notes: No form of money scores perfectly: gold is durable and scarce but hard to divide and transport for small payments, while paper cash is portable but can be damaged and its supply can expand. Think critically: ask learners to name a time they chose one type of money or asset over another, and which properties mattered most to them, and why. -->

---

## Barter and the Double Coincidence of Wants

* Barter is the direct exchange of one good or service for another.
* It works in small exchanges but struggles with many people and products.
* Double coincidence of wants: each must want what the other offers, at once.
* Goods may spoil or be indivisible, with no common unit for prices.
* Commonly accepted money separates selling from buying.

<!-- notes: Walk through the guide's example: Amina has rice and wants shoes, Luis has shoes but wants cooking oil, and Mei has cooking oil but wants repair work. Ask the class how Amina could get her shoes, then show how money lets her sell rice to one person and buy shoes from another. -->

---

## Scarcity, Value, and Exchange

* Scarcity: choosing one use of a resource prevents another use.
* Value is not inside the object; it depends on people and circumstances.
* Exchange happens when both sides prefer what they receive.
* Opportunity cost, time preference, and trade-offs shape everyday choices.
* Scarcity alone does not guarantee value: demand matters too.

<!-- notes: Use the guide's clean water example: inexpensive near a safe public source, extremely valuable during a shortage. Ask learners for an opportunity cost from their own week, such as money spent on transport that cannot also be spent on food. -->

---

## Beginner Monetary History From Barter to Digital Money

* Money did not develop in one identical sequence everywhere.
* Early exchange relied on barter, gifts, obligations, and local records of debts.
* Commodity money: salt, beads, shells, livestock, copper, silver, gold.
* Standard coins helped accounting, but authorities could debase them.

<!-- notes: Present this as a broad pattern, not a universal sequence. You can read the guide's quotation from Carl Menger ("On the Origin of Money", 1892), which describes money as the unintended product of historical development rather than an agreement. -->

---

## From Paper Claims to Digital Money

* Paper claims on metal were easier to move but required trusting the issuer.
* Gold standards legally linked a currency unit to a stated amount of gold.
* Bank and government money came to rest on records and promises.
* Most money today moves as digital records; a card or phone is an interface.
* Convenience grew, but so did dependence on issuers and intermediaries.

<!-- notes: Emphasise the repeated trade-off: each step added convenience while increasing reliance on monetary policy, issuers, banks, and payment intermediaries. Point out that national currency becoming no longer redeemable for gold leads directly into the next slide on fiat money. -->

---

## Fiat Money

* Issued under government authority, not redeemable for a fixed amount of a commodity.
* "Fiat" is Latin for "let it be done", often understood as "by decree".
* Laws typically make it legal tender and require it for taxes.
* Network effects: people want it because others use it.
* Central banks, commercial banks, governments, and citizens each play a role.

<!-- notes: Local scenario activity: learners choose one ordinary payment in their community (or a realistic invented one) and explain in three or four sentences what is paid for and in which currency, why the recipient accepts that currency, and which institutions, records, or rules the payment relies on. Finish by naming one way the payment would become harder if shared acceptance or trust broke down. -->

---

## The Trade-Offs of Fiat Money

* Offers convenient payments, flexible credit, and tools for financial crises.
* That flexibility creates risk: a few institutions can change supply and cost.
* Institutions can restrict access or reduce purchasing power.
* Users trust banks, payment services, central banks, and governments.
* These rules are not fixed and can be open to neglect or abuse.

<!-- notes: Keep the tone balanced: the guide notes these systems can work for long periods. Ask the class which layer of trust they rely on most in a normal week, and what would happen if it failed. -->

---

## Fractional Reserve Banking

* A deposit is the bank's liability: it owes you the balance.
* Banks keep only a fraction of deposits as immediately accessible reserves.
* A new loan usually creates new deposit money.
* Liquidity and solvency risks can trigger a bank run.
* A bank balance is the bank's promise to pay you.

<!-- notes: Emphasise the central lesson: not that every bank is about to fail, but that a bank balance is not the same as holding the settlement asset directly. Mention that deposit insurance and central-bank lending can reduce panic, but eligibility, coverage, and rules vary by jurisdiction. -->

---

## Inflation and Purchasing Power

* Price inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level.
* Purchasing power: the goods and services a unit of money can buy.
* A basket rising from 100 to 110 units: 100 units no longer buy it.
* Causes are many and debated; avoid single-factor explanations.
* Inflation affects savers, borrowers, lenders, and workers differently.

<!-- notes: Purchasing-power check: each learner records today's price of one ordinary local item, asks what it cost several years ago, and checks whether income changed at the same rate. Then compare it with an official inflation measure and its basket, noting that a change in method is not automatically manipulation but should be documented transparently. -->

---

## Why the US Dollar Is a World Reserve Currency

* A reserve currency is held by central banks for international payments.
* Before the First World War, the British pound led this role.
* 1944 Bretton Woods: currencies linked to the dollar, the dollar to gold.
* In 1971, President Nixon ended official exchange of dollars for gold.
* The dollar remains the leading reserve currency, though its share can change.

<!-- notes: Ask why the dollar stayed dominant after 1971. The guide gives three reasons: its established role in trade and finance, the size of the U.S. economy, and the depth of U.S. financial markets. -->

---

## Why People Look for Alternatives

* People seek alternatives when they expect cash to lose purchasing power.
* Common choices: precious metals, real estate, and stocks.
* Each has trade-offs: storage, entry cost, volatility, or custody.
* No asset guarantees purchasing power in every period and situation.
* Bitcoin emerged as another proposed alternative, without a central issuer.

<!-- notes: Stress that these assets are not identical to money (a home may store value but cannot buy food) and that every choice carries opportunity cost and risk. Remind learners that tax rules vary by jurisdiction and this course does not provide tax advice; Bitcoin's claim is something the course will evaluate, not assume. -->

---

## Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs)

* A CBDC is digital fiat money issued as a central bank liability.
* It differs from physical cash and from a commercial-bank deposit.
* Designs differ: some for institutions, others proposed for public use.
* It may speed settlement, but can also enable monitoring and limits.
* Digital does not mean decentralized, scarce, private, or verifiable.

<!-- notes: Emphasise that a CBDC does not create a fixed supply or remove central control: the central bank remains the issuer and can still apply monetary policy. It changes the technology and distribution of fiat money. -->

---

<!-- _class: two-column -->

## Questions to Ask About a CBDC

* Who issues the money?
* Who can hold or use it?
* What information is collected?
* Can a payment be blocked, limited, or reversed?
* Can the rules change?
* Can ordinary users verify the rules?
* What happens without electricity or internet?
* What legal protections exist?

<!-- notes: Ask small groups to apply these questions to a CBDC discussed in their own region. Remind them not to assume every proposed CBDC has the same design, and to use current, local, and reliable information. -->

---

## Review: How Can Money Break?

1. What makes the money you use useful in daily life?
1. Which of its rules depend on institutions you must trust?
1. How can money break?

<!-- notes: Give learners a quiet minute to answer, then discuss as a group. Close with the guide's summary: barter is difficult because matching needs is inefficient, fiat money makes exchange easier but can involve changing supply, restricted access, and declining purchasing power, and people look for alternatives when they want different trade-offs; the next module examines Bitcoin's attempt to create a different set of monetary rules. -->

---

# Module 2: How Bitcoin Works

You will explain why Bitcoin was created and what makes it different.

---

## Why Bitcoin Was Created

* Earlier digital payments relied on a trusted organization to keep the ledger
* Intermediaries can delay, charge, restrict, reverse, or fail; issuers can change supply
* Digital money was hard: data copies easily, and a central guard becomes a control point
* Bitcoin lets a peer-to-peer network agree on one history without a central ledger keeper
* "Bitcoin" means the network; "bitcoin" means the unit

<!-- notes: Emphasise that Bitcoin reduces the need to trust a central authority for issuing and settling its money; it does not remove trust from every part of life. Ask the class: which payment intermediaries do you rely on, and what could they do to your payment? Point out that a wallet or exchange may connect to Bitcoin but is not the Bitcoin network itself. -->

---

## The Search for Digital and Decentralized Money

* The Cypherpunks explored how cryptography could protect privacy and freedom online
* Cryptography uses math to keep information private and prove an action is authentic
* Digital signatures prove who authorized something without revealing the secret key
* Private communication was not enough: people also needed to exchange value online
* The real goal: decentralized digital money, with no single issuer, server, or administrator

<!-- notes: Point out that bank deposits were already digital, so the hard part was removing the trusted issuer, not making money digital. Remind the class that Bitcoin built on decades of work by many people. Ask: if every online payment needs a bank or company, what can that intermediary do? -->

---

## Early Digital-Money Attempts and the Double-Spend Problem

* DigiCash: private digital payments, but it depended on one company and failed with it
* Hashcash: proof of work made unwanted email costly to send
* Bit Gold, b-money, reusable proof of work: ideas on digital scarcity, but incomplete
* BitTorrent: not money, but showed peer-to-peer sharing without a central server
* The open problem: double spending, because digital data copies perfectly

<!-- notes: Stress that Bitcoin drew on four decades of experimentation, and each project contributed a piece of the puzzle. Ask the class: why can a paper note not be given to two people at once, while a digital file can? -->

---

## How the Whitepaper Answered Double Spending

* 31 October 2008: Satoshi Nakamoto published _Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System_
* Peer-to-peer network: transactions are announced directly to participants
* Digital signatures: only the private key holder can authorize a spend
* Blocks and mining: miners compete with proof of work to add the next block
* Nodes verify the rules; the valid chain with the most accumulated work is the shared record

<!-- notes: Centralized systems prevent double spending by letting one trusted database decide which payment came first; the whitepaper combined these features to reach the same result without a central authority. Tell the class that the rest of the module explains each feature in more detail. -->

---

## Digital Scarcity and Credible Scarcity

* Digital information is normally easy to copy
* Digital scarcity: the system rejects invalid copies and spending the same unit twice
* Bitcoin combines signatures, a public history, proof of work, and rule verification
* Scarcity is credible when rules are public and independently verifiable
* No single issuer can quietly change the supply for everyone

<!-- notes: Emphasise the difference between scarcity that is claimed and scarcity that can be checked. Point ahead: this is why Bitcoin's supply limit is studied together with decentralization and node verification. -->

---

## The Blockchain - Bitcoin’s Public Ledger

* Banks each keep their own ledger; Bitcoin uses one shared, public, decentralized ledger
* A block is like a page of transactions in precise chronological order
* Blocks are chained together in order, forming the "blockchain"
* A new block is added approximately every 10 minutes
* The ledger is append only, so it is considered "immutable"

<!-- notes: Use the image of a ledger book: each block is a page, and pages are only ever added in order. Ask the class why being unable to alter past records matters for accuracy, security, and stability. -->

---

## Decentralization

* Centralized systems have a controlling point: a bank, payment company, or government
* Bitcoin has no central office to create accounts, change balances, or approve membership
* Open-source software, independent nodes, competing miners, and many copies of the ledger
* Rule changes require voluntary adoption by participants
* Trade-offs: slower coordination, more user responsibility, no central support desk

<!-- notes: Stress that decentralization is not equal influence: mining equipment, technical knowledge, money, and internet access are unevenly distributed. Bitcoin's claim is narrower: no single participant can unilaterally change the ledger or monetary rules for everyone. Ask: what does a person lose when there is no support desk to call? -->

---

## Digital Signatures

* A wallet uses a private key to sign and authorize a transaction
* Only the holder of the required private key can authorize the spend
* The network verifies the signature without learning the private key
* No bank or central operator has to give permission
* Whoever obtains the key can usually spend; a lost key may mean lost bitcoin

<!-- notes: Emphasise that control of the private key is critical; this sets up self-custody in the next module. Ask the class: what is the difference between a wallet application and the private key it manages? -->

---

## Peer-to-Peer Transfer

* Participants interact through a network without a central coordinator
* A user signs a transaction and broadcasts it; no Bitcoin operator approves it
* The recipient does not need an account at the sender's bank
* Users can still choose intermediaries, but then depend on their rules and security
* A valid payment to the wrong address usually cannot be reversed

<!-- notes: Clarify that peer-to-peer does not mean only two computers talk directly: a transaction may pass through wallets, internet providers, and many nodes. The custody choice determines who has practical control. Ask where peer-to-peer transfer could help people in different places or using different banking systems. -->

---

## Users, Nodes, and Miners

* Users receive, hold, and send bitcoin, with their own keys or a custodian
* Nodes independently verify the rules and reject invalid data
* Miners build candidate blocks and compete through proof of work
* Developers propose software changes; participants choose whether to run them
* The roles limit one another: no single role controls the network

<!-- notes: Ask the guide's spot question: why does a node verify transactions, and what is at least one rule it checks? Good answers include valid signatures, bitcoin not already spent, proof of work, and the issuance schedule. Note that one person can hold several roles, such as a miner who also runs a node. -->

---

## Proof of Work

* Miners repeatedly hash a candidate block, searching for a result below a target
* There is no shortcut: finding a result is costly, checking it is quick
* Each valid block added after a transaction adds another confirmation
* Difficulty adjusts so blocks arrive about every ten minutes on average
* The chain with the most accumulated work is the shared history

<!-- notes: Emphasise "costly to produce, easy to verify", and that mining secures the ordering of transactions rather than solving equations for an outside purpose. On energy, present both the supporters' and the critics' views, then ask what a responsible evaluation should compare: what energy, from where, for what security, and against which alternatives. -->

---

## Finite Supply

* Maximum supply: 21 million bitcoin, issued through the block subsidy to miners
* Every 210,000 blocks the subsidy halves (a "halving"), roughly every four years
* The subsidy began at 50 bitcoin per block; since the 2024 halving it is 3.125
* Nodes enforce the limit by rejecting blocks that create too much bitcoin
* A network rule, not a physical law: it holds while participants enforce it

<!-- notes: Ask the guide's spot questions: what are the advantages of a limited supply, what would increasing the supply do to purchasing power, and who would reject an increase? Remind the class that finite supply does not guarantee a rising price, since price also depends on demand. -->

---

## Satoshis and Unit Bias

* One bitcoin divides into 100,000,000 satoshis (sats)
* A sat is the smallest unit recorded on Bitcoin's base layer
* You do not need to buy or use a whole bitcoin
* Unit bias: wrongly feeling that a low price per unit means cheaper or more accessible
* 100,000 sats and 0.001 bitcoin are the same amount

<!-- notes: Run the guide's "Show your understanding" check: how many sats are in 0.01 bitcoin (1,000,000), and why does the price of one whole bitcoin not prevent someone from using a smaller amount? Encourage learners to compare share of total supply, value involved, and risks, not the price per unit. -->

---

<!-- _class: two-column -->

## Bitcoin as Sound Digital Money

* Durability: the ledger is copied across many nodes
* Portability: value moves over a communications network
* Divisibility: 100,000,000 sats per bitcoin
* Fungibility: equal amounts spend under the same rules
* Scarcity: public issuance rules, 21 million maximum
* Verifiability: a node can check supply and ownership
* Acceptability: growing in some places, still limited

<!-- notes: Define sound money as money whose supply is difficult to expand and whose properties support reliable use across time; stress that it is a judgment based on criteria, to be examined rather than accepted as a label. Note two caveats from the guide: durability depends on preserving valid keys, and public transaction history can affect how some services treat particular funds. -->

---

## Functions of Money: Benefits and Trade-Offs

* Store of value: predictable supply, but a volatile market price
* Medium of exchange: moves without a bank; acceptance, fees, and tools vary
* Unit of account: amounts in bitcoin or sats, but most goods are priced in fiat
* Benefits: direct ownership, predictable supply, global transfer, open participation
* Trade-offs: volatility, security responsibility, irreversible errors, changing fees, limited acceptance

<!-- notes: Run the guide's local-value check: does bitcoin have value in your community? Ask learners to separate three questions: what market price can be observed, where (if anywhere) is it accepted, and what personal preference or bias affects their judgment. Encourage comparing Bitcoin with fiat money, precious metals, and other assets using the same standards. -->

---

## Bitcoin, Stablecoins, and Other Crypto-Assets

* Digital assets can look alike on screen but rely on very different systems
* Bitcoin: public supply rules, with no issuer creating bitcoin outside them
* Stablecoins: designed to track another value; rely on issuers, reserves, and counterparties
* Other digital assets: issuance, control, and what you can verify vary by asset
* The comparison only clarifies Bitcoin; it is not a recommendation

<!-- notes: Emphasise that the Diploma does not recommend acquiring, trading, or promoting any asset; the names and tickers in the guide are identifiers only, not endorsements. Mention that a stablecoin can lose its intended value, and issuers or platforms may freeze tokens where their rules or laws allow. -->

---

## Questions to Ask About Any Digital Asset

* Who controls issuance or changes to the rules?
* What can I verify independently?
* Who holds the keys?
* Which company, issuer, or other counterparty must I trust?
* Am I judging the asset itself, or the service holding it for me?

<!-- notes: Ask the class to apply these questions to Bitcoin and then to a stablecoin, and compare the answers. Stress that a familiar ticker, a stable-looking price, or a well-designed app does not remove the need to compare custody, counterparty, technical, legal, and loss risks. -->

---

## Learning and Responsible Use Are Not Trading or Promotion

* Learning how Bitcoin works differs from chasing short-term price changes
* Trading and speculation can bring rapid decisions, loss, and platform risk
* Token promotion can create conflicts of interest
* This Diploma helps you explain Bitcoin, ask better questions, and decide more safely
* It gives no investment advice on what to buy, sell, or hold

<!-- notes: Set this expectation clearly, especially if learners arrive expecting tips. Ask the class: how can you tell education apart from promotion when someone talks about a digital asset? -->

---

## Bitcoin Origin Timeline

* 31 October 2008: Satoshi Nakamoto publishes the Bitcoin whitepaper
* 3 January 2009: the Genesis Block starts the Bitcoin network
* 12 January 2009: first recorded person-to-person transaction, to Hal Finney
* 22 May 2010: 10,000 bitcoin paid for two pizzas (Bitcoin Pizza Day)
* Satoshi's identity remains unknown; no central company owns Bitcoin

<!-- notes: Mention that the Genesis Block included a newspaper headline about bank bailouts, placing the launch in the context of the financial crisis, but encourage learners to evaluate Bitcoin by its design and operation, not only its origin story. If you have internet access, show the whitepaper and the Genesis Block on a block explorer, noting that an explorer is an interface to public data, not an authority over Bitcoin's rules. -->

---

## Participant Empowerment, Risk, and Responsibility

* Public rules, a peer-to-peer network, and signatures give participants meaningful roles
* No single role controls the network for everyone
* No central authority can recover access or reverse many errors
* Self-custody: no company can freeze your bitcoin, or restore a lost seed phrase
* Responsibility is not acting alone: use education, peer review, and small tests

<!-- notes: Stress that self-custody means protecting backups, verifying addresses, checking wallet behaviour, and creating a recovery plan; the next module covers how. Remind learners that asking for help must never mean giving anyone access to secret information. -->

---

## Review: How Does Bitcoin Respond to Fiat Problems?

1. How do public rules, finite issuance, peer-to-peer transfer, and verification answer fiat concerns?
1. How do the roles of nodes and miners differ?
1. Why must double spending be prevented?
1. What new responsibilities arise when a person controls their own keys?
1. Does Bitcoin have value to you? Does its current price represent its value?

<!-- notes: Let learners think about each question on their own first, then discuss in pairs or as a whole group, linking answers back to the fiat problems from the previous module. Close with the guide's summary: Bitcoin replaces some institutional trust with verification and personal responsibility; it does not remove risk or trade-offs. -->

# Module 3: Using Bitcoin Safely

Explain who controls the keys, receive or send safely, respond to scams, and show recovery readiness.

---

## Three Rules for Safe First Steps

* Share receiving information, never secrets
* Pause before approving a send
* Keep mistakes small: stop and ask when a request is unclear or rushed
* Practice uses a simulation, test funds, or an educator-approved amount
* You never need to buy bitcoin to complete the Diploma

<!-- notes: Stress that the goal is not speed, it is knowing when to pause. Introduce the module's three questions: what can I share, what must I check before I receive or send, and when should I stop and ask for help? Tell learners these prepare them for three guided practices: a receive-screen check, a send-and-verify check, and a recovery-readiness or scam-response scenario. -->

---

## Wallets

* A wallet manages the information needed to receive and authorize transactions
* Bitcoin is recorded on the shared ledger, not stored inside a phone
* The first question about any wallet: who controls the keys?
* Check who can authorize a payment and how access is recovered
* Start with your educator's prepared environment, not a stranger's recommendation

<!-- notes: Ask the class: what would happen if your phone stopped working? The record would still exist, but access depends on the keys and recovery method the wallet uses. Mention that well-established open-source wallet software is preferred where appropriate, but open source alone does not prove a tool is safe or suitable. -->

---

## Addresses

* An address is public receiving information for an on-chain payment
* It is not a password and cannot authorize spending by itself
* Take it from the intended wallet's receive screen: scan or copy, never retype
* After copying, compare the destination with the trusted receiving screen
* An on-chain address and a Lightning invoice are different

<!-- notes: Ask: why check after copying? Malicious software can replace copied address information. Emphasise that an address is never safe just because it looks familiar: confirm recipient, network, destination and payment method, and use a small test first when the process or amount is unfamiliar. A new address for a new receipt can also improve privacy. -->

---

## Keys

* A private key is secret: it allows a wallet to authorize spending
* A public key helps verify a signature without revealing the private key
* A signature proves authorization; the private key is not inside the transaction
* Beginners should not copy or handle individual private-key strings
* No legitimate educator, company, or developer needs your private key or seed phrase

<!-- notes: Write the guide's essential rule on the board: "Public receiving information can be shared. Private spending information must remain secret." Anyone who asks for a private key or seed phrase should be treated as a threat. Try it: learners sort prepared examples into safe to share for receiving, secret spending information, and public information that should still be kept private when linked to a person. -->

---

## Seed Phrases

* An ordered list of words (commonly 12 or 24) that backs up a wallet's keys
* Order and spelling matter
* Whoever has it can often restore the wallet and spend: it is full access
* Keep it offline; never photograph, email, message, or type it into a website
* If it may have been seen or copied, treat it as compromised

<!-- notes: Go through the full protection list: write it down privately and accurately, follow the wallet's official backup instructions, and keep it out of cameras, microphones, other people's view, and places where it may be damaged, lost or found. If compromised, seek guidance without revealing it and use a new trusted recovery process. Passphrases and multiple-key setups are Advanced learning: do not add complexity you cannot explain and recover from. -->

---

## Custodial and Non-Custodial Use

* Custody asks: who controls the keys required to spend the bitcoin?
* Custodial: a company or another person controls the keys
* A custodial balance is a promise from the custodian
* Self-custodial: you control the keys and handle recovery through a backup
* If a company must approve your withdrawal, it is probably custodial

<!-- notes: Name the trade-off: familiar support with provider dependence, versus direct control with backup and error responsibility. Stress that self-custody is not a test of personal worth; the important step is identifying the trust and risk involved. Try it: learners read two prepared wallet descriptions and state who controls the keys, who could restore access, and one risk to check. -->

---

## Hot and Cold Wallets

* A hot wallet keeps keys on an internet-connected device, such as a phone
* Hot wallets are convenient for regular payments but have a larger attack surface
* A cold wallet keeps signing keys away from internet-connected general-purpose devices
* A hardware wallet is a dedicated device that keeps keys isolated
* The device, backup, and recovery process still need protection

<!-- notes: Hot wallet risks include malicious software, insecure updates, phishing, physical access and device compromise. A custodial account offers familiar login and support, but the provider controls access and may impose limits. Try it: for a small fictional payment and a less frequent fictional payment, learners explain which trade-off matters more, without choosing a tool or amount for their own funds. -->

---

## Wallet Recovery and Seed Phrase Testing

* A recovery plan restores the expected wallet without exposing recovery information
* Practice only with educator-prepared, empty, or simulated materials
* Follow the wallet's official recovery method, privately, away from cameras
* Confirm the expected training wallet, then reset the temporary material
* Never enter a real recovery phrase into a website, message, or form

<!-- notes: Walk through the six steps: official instructions, confirm compatibility, work privately, use the approved backup-check or recovery method, confirm the expected receiving information or history, reset the environment. Explain that you can observe their process without seeing any recovery words. The point is to understand the process before a device failure creates pressure. -->

---

## Receiving Bitcoin

* Receiving cannot send funds out, but it still needs careful checking
* Use the intended wallet and identify on-chain or Lightning
* Share only the address, QR code, or payment request
* A message or screenshot saying "sent" is not proof: check your own wallet
* Confirmations add confidence but do not repair a wrong address or scam

<!-- notes: This is Practice 1: receive safely. Have learners open the prepared wallet, choose Receive, identify the payment method, share only receiving information, check any amount with the sender, and check status in their own wallet. Ask: what evidence can you see yourself? -->

---

## Sending Bitcoin

* A valid Bitcoin transaction is usually irreversible
* Confirm the recipient, purpose, and payment method through a trusted channel
* Compare the destination with the trusted receiving screen
* Check the amount, unit, fee or expiry, and final summary
* Use a small test when the process is unfamiliar

<!-- notes: This is Practice 2: pause and check before sending. Ask before anyone approves: can you explain who will receive the payment and why? The on-chain network fee depends mainly on transaction data and demand for block space, not the amount, so read the final summary. If the destination changes, the request becomes urgent, or anyone asks for recovery information: stop and verify independently. -->

---

## Verification, Block Explorers, and Irreversible Transactions

* A transaction ID (TXID) identifies an on-chain transaction
* A block explorer shows public data: status, destination, amount, fee, confirmations
* It cannot prove why a payment happened or whether someone is trustworthy
* Later confirmations add confidence but do not fix a wrong destination
* Searching an address or TXID may reveal your interest to the explorer's operator

<!-- notes: Ask: what can this public information show, and what can it not show? There is no universal confirmation count; the suitable check depends on the amount and risk. For practice, use an educator-prepared transaction or printed screenshot, and never search a learner's personal address or transaction on a public service. -->

## Safety Checklist

* Someone impersonates support or an educator
* Pressure to act urgently
* Requests for secrecy
* Promises of guaranteed returns
* Asking you to send bitcoin first
* A changed destination
* Requests for keys or recovery words
* Requests for remote access or an advance fee

<!-- notes: This is Practice 3: spot a risk and stop safely. Ask the class: what would make you stop before a payment or installation? Treat every item on this slide as a red flag. -->

---

## The Safe Response to a Red Flag

* Pause. Share nothing. Send nothing. Close the channel.
* Verify independently through a known source
* Ask for help without revealing secrets
* Use official instructions; beware sponsored results, message links, and look-alike sites
* Start with a simulation or small approved amount; never borrow to experiment

<!-- notes: Emphasise that a real opportunity can survive a careful check, while a scam often depends on preventing one. If a process differs from the demonstrated one, pause rather than solving it under pressure. Try it: in a prepared message or payment screen, learners identify the warning sign, what could be lost, and the first safe action. -->

---

## Acquiring and Exchanging Bitcoin

* Pathway awareness, not financial advice
* Pathways include earning, peer-to-peer exchange, exchanges, brokers, ATMs, and kiosks
* Each has different cost, custody, privacy, fraud, and legal trade-offs
* This Diploma does not recommend a provider or path
* KYC identity checks can help recovery but link identity to financial activity

<!-- notes: Before comparing a path, have learners ask about costs, custody, identity and privacy, withdrawal and settlement, counterparty safety, and whether it is lawful and appropriate where they live. Stress that no pathway is automatically best everywhere; local examples and provider-specific guidance belong in Module 4. -->

---

## On-Chain and Lightning Payments

* On-chain payments are recorded in Bitcoin's blockchain and gain confirmations
* Lightning is a payment network built on Bitcoin for fast, frequent payments
* A Lightning payment usually completes or fails quickly
* A Lightning wallet can still be custodial or self-custodial
* Confirm which path the recipient expects before paying

<!-- notes: An on-chain wallet cannot directly pay every Lightning invoice, and a Lightning wallet may not support every on-chain action. Ask: in a scenario offering both paths, which request can the intended wallet use, and what would you check before approving it? Lightning setup, channels and routing are Advanced learning; live local practice belongs in Module 4. -->

## Practical Competency Check Observed by an Educator

* Your educator observes you in a prepared, simulated, or low-stakes environment
* Navigate the prepared wallet and check receiving information
* Complete or safely simulate a receive or send
* Show recovery readiness while keeping all secrets private
* Not ready yet? You receive feedback, more practice, and another opportunity

<!-- notes: Make sure learners have had supported practice and know the task and safety boundaries before assessment. The observation is yours to make, not an app's, an attendance record's, or a self-report. Learners may also practise responding safely to a mismatch or scam through guided scenarios. -->

1. How do you protect a recovery plan?

## Invoices, Addresses and a Guided Payment

* A Lightning invoice is a request for one specific payment
* A Lightning address looks like an email but depends on a provider
* Check recipient, amount, unit and fee before approving
* Both sides confirm the result in their own wallets
* Payment convenience and custody security are separate questions

## KYC and No-KYC Trade-Offs

* KYC may meet local rules, connect to banks and support recovery
* KYC also creates lasting data risk from breaches or policy changes
* No-KYC may improve privacy but bring less support and more fraud risk
* Privacy is the ability to control information about yourself
* Never break local law or risk physical danger for privacy

<!-- notes: Only cover this where locally appropriate, and check the current local rules beforehand. Learners can compare a real KYC path and no-KYC path with the questions in their guide. When rules are unclear, direct learners to a qualified local person. -->

## Circular Economies

* People earn, spend, save and exchange bitcoin within a community
* Can reduce dependence on distant services and build local relationships
* Can fail with little demand, poor tools, weak security or pressure
* Healthy loops are voluntary and solve real problems for several participants
* Adoption numbers alone do not show usefulness

<!-- notes: Have learners map a simple local cycle with the guide's seven questions, including what happens when internet access fails. Stress that a strong loop should not depend permanently on giveaways or on one organizer. -->

## Merchant Use Cases

* A merchant needs more than a receive button
* Decide pricing units, on-chain or Lightning, and custody model
* Plan device access, conversion, confirmation and refunds
* Know the required records and what staff do when systems fail

<!-- notes: If you can, invite a local merchant to describe their real process. Define success by the problem solved, not by the number of transactions completed. -->

## Community Use Cases and Shared Custody

* Pay local workers or creators and receive donations transparently
* Support savings groups, remote workers and local producers
* Provide emergency support where ordinary payments are unavailable
* An organizer holding funds for others becomes a custodian
* Shared funds need clear rules, records and access controls

<!-- notes: Ask learners which use cases fit your community; do not supply examples the guide does not give. Emphasise that shared funds may need legal review where appropriate. -->

## Local Resource Maps and Community Discovery

* Maps and directories help find merchants, educators and meetups
* Listings can be incomplete or outdated
* Contact before a special journey and bring another way to pay
* Never reveal seed phrases, balances or home addresses at meetups
* Good communities respect your choices without one-leader dependence
