# 1.9 Inflation and purchasing power

**Price inflation** is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services. If your income and savings do not rise as quickly as prices, your money buys less.

**Purchasing power** is the amount of goods and services a unit of money can buy. If a basket of basic goods rises from 100 units of currency to 110, the same 100 units no longer buy the full basket. Its purchasing power in relation to that basket has fallen.

Inflation can have several causes. These include an expansion of money and credit, supply disruptions, changes in demand, currency weakness, taxes, regulation, and changes in production costs. The causes and effects are often debated. Avoid explanations that assign every price change to one factor.

Inflation does not affect everyone equally. A person who holds mainly cash may lose purchasing power. A person who owns an asset that rises in price may be partly protected. Borrowers may repay fixed debts with money that buys less, while lenders may receive less real value than expected. Wages and prices also adjust at different speeds.

The rate of inflation is an average. Your personal experience may differ because your spending is not identical to the measured basket. Food, housing, transport, education, and health costs may change at different rates.

**Purchasing-power check:** Choose one ordinary item in your community. Record its price today. Ask what it cost several years ago, then check a reliable record if possible. Did income change at the same rate? What other factors may explain the change?

Then compare your item with an official inflation measure. Which goods and services are in that measure's basket? Has the basket or method changed over time, and does the source explain why? A change is not automatically manipulation, but it can affect comparisons and should be documented transparently.
