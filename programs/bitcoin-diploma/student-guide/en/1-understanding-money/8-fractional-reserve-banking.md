# 1.8 Fractional reserve banking

When you deposit cash in a bank, the bank records a [liability](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#liability) to you: it owes you the account balance. The bank does not normally keep every unit of every deposit as physical cash. Instead, it keeps [liquid reserves](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#liquidity)—cash or [assets](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#asset) it can use quickly for payments—and uses its balance sheet to make loans and other investments.

When a commercial bank approves a loan, it usually creates a new deposit in the borrower's account. This expands the amount of bank [deposit money](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#bank-deposit). When the principal is repaid, that [deposit money](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#bank-deposit) is reduced. Banks are limited by [capital](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#capital) rules, [liquidity](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#liquidity) needs, [credit risk](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#credit-risk), regulation, the cost of funding, and demand from [qualified borrowers](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#qualified-borrower).

Banks generally seek [profit](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#bank-profit) by earning interest and fees on loans and investments while paying their operating, funding, and regulatory costs and absorbing losses when borrowers do not repay. These activities and constraints differ by bank and jurisdiction.


<!-- card: definition -->
> **Definition – Fractional reserve banking**
>
> [Fractional reserve banking](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#fractional-reserve-banking) describes banking in which banks hold only a fraction of their deposit obligations as immediately accessible reserves while making loans and holding other [assets](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#asset). The required reserve level and other limits differ between countries and can change over time.


This creates two important risks:

* **[Liquidity risk](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#liquidity-risk):** A bank may own long-term loans that are expected to be repaid but still lack enough cash or quickly usable [assets](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#asset) for many withdrawals today.
* **[Solvency risk](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#solvency-risk):** Failed loans or falling [asset](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#asset) [values](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#value) may leave the bank's total [assets](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#asset) worth less than what it owes. In that case, the bank cannot fully meet its obligations even if it has time to sell [assets](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#asset).

A [**bank run**](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#bank-run) happens when many depositors try to withdraw their money or account balances at the same time because they fear the bank may fail. [Deposit insurance](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#deposit-insurance) and [central-bank](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#central-bank) lending can reduce panic, but insurance eligibility and coverage limits vary by jurisdiction. These protections depend on institutions and rules, and they can move some losses or risks from an individual bank to the wider system.

Regulation and oversight can reduce risk, but they cannot prevent every case of fraud, poor management, political abuse, or simple error. These failures are possibilities to evaluate, not outcomes of every banking system.

The central lesson is not that every bank is about to fail. It is that a bank balance is the bank's promise to pay you. It is not the same as holding the settlement [asset](https://curriculum.myfirstbitcoin.org/lexicon/en/#asset) directly.
