# 1.8 Bank balance sheets and lending

_Role play, Simulation · Whole class, Small groups · 35 min_

Participants build a simple bank balance sheet with cards or a shared table. They compare a customer deposit, a bank loan that creates a deposit balance, and a reserve balance. The model is intentionally limited. It does not describe every banking system, establish a current reserve rule, measure a national money supply, or predict bank failure.

#### Objectives

* Participants distinguish deposits, loans, and reserves in a simplified bank balance sheet and identify questions that require local, current evidence.

#### Materials

* A whiteboard, paper chart, or projected table.
* Three sets of cards labelled “deposit,” “loan,” “reserve,” and “cash withdrawal request,” or equivalent paper labels.
* Calculators, if useful.

#### Setup

Draw this table before class:


| Event | Bank asset | Bank liability | Customer balance | Question to discuss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Customer deposit | Cash or reserve balance increases | Deposit liability increases | Customer A has a deposit | What does the bank owe the customer? |
| Bank makes a loan | Loan asset increases | Deposit liability increases | Customer B has a deposit | What conditions affect a bank's ability to lend? |
| Payment or withdrawal | Asset and deposit balances may change | Deposit liability may change | Depends on the payment route | What liquidity or settlement questions arise? |


Explain that “asset” and “liability” describe the bank's balance sheet. A deposit is generally a liability of the bank because the bank owes the depositor. A loan is generally an asset of the bank because the borrower owes the bank.

#### Procedure

##### Step 1: Record a customer deposit

Place the deposit card in the first row. Ask participants to identify what the customer has and what the bank owes. Record the corresponding asset and liability entries without calling the result a measure of the whole money supply.

##### Step 2: Record a bank loan

Present a fictional approved loan. Add a loan asset and a deposit liability to the table. Explain that commercial-bank lending can create a deposit balance, but lending decisions also depend on borrowers, bank capital, liquidity, funding, risk, profitability, regulation, and local conditions.

##### Step 3: Compare payment and settlement routes

Use a fictional payment from Customer B to Customer C at another bank. Ask what balances may need to change and why banks need liquidity to settle payments. Do not ask learners to calculate a multiplier.

##### Step 4: Test a withdrawal scenario

Give the class a card stating that several customers want cash or transfers at the same time. Ask what information would be needed before judging the bank's liquidity or solvency. Distinguish a question about ready cash and settlement from a question about whether assets exceed liabilities.

##### Step 5: Compare the model with local evidence

Ask participants to list facts that cannot be inferred from this activity, such as local reserve rules, deposit insurance, capital requirements, supervision, or recent bank conditions. Explain that an educator must use current local authoritative sources before discussing those facts. The [Fractional Reserve Explorer](https://tools.myfirstbitcoin.org/fractional-reserve-explorer) shows a country's banking capital and reserve rules and can start the comparison; confirm the current figures with the central bank or banking regulator before presenting them.

#### Key points

1. A bank deposit is a liability of the bank, while a loan is generally an asset of the bank.
1. Lending, deposits, reserves, liquidity, capital, and money measures are related but different concepts.
1. A classroom balance sheet is not a complete model of a banking system or a prediction of bank failure.
1. Claims about local reserve rules, deposit insurance, bailouts, bank runs, and incentives need current, local evidence.

#### Discussion questions

1. Why does the bank record a customer deposit as a liability?
1. What changes when a bank makes a loan and credits a customer's deposit balance?
1. What information would you need before deciding whether a bank has a liquidity or solvency problem?
1. Which claims about banking must be checked for the local jurisdiction and date?

#### Notes

> **Dark – This is a simplified balance-sheet model**
>
> Banks operate under different laws, regulations, funding arrangements, capital requirements, liquidity needs, and payment systems. This activity introduces terms and relationships. It does not show that a fixed reserve ratio determines lending or that one cause explains a bank run, default, bailout, or moral hazard.


This activity is formative. It may prepare learners for a money-and-banking discussion, but it is not evidence of a financial, legal, accounting, or investment competency.
